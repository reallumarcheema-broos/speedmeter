How much internet speed do I actually need?

Updated 22 September 2026 · {{SITE_NAME}}

Internet plans are sold on one number, and that number is almost always bigger than anything you will use. Here is what each activity actually consumes, and how to work out the plan your household needs.

What individual activities use

These are sustained download figures for one stream, taken from the providers' own published requirements. They are steady-state numbers — a video player will grab more than this in bursts to fill its buffer, then idle.

Two things stand out. First, gaming barely uses any bandwidth while you are playing — it is latency-sensitive, not speed-sensitive, which is a different problem covered in ping, jitter and packet loss. Second, 4K video is the single heaviest routine activity in most homes.

Sizing a household

The naive method is to add up every device you own. That over-buys badly, because people do not all stream 4K simultaneously. A better rule: take your two heaviest simultaneous activities, add them, then double the total for headroom.

Worked examples:

  • One or two people, HD streaming, some browsing. Heaviest pair: 8 + 5 = 13 Mbps. Doubled: ~25 Mbps.
  • Family of four, one 4K TV, someone on a video call, kids on tablets. Heaviest pair: 25 + 4 = 29 Mbps. Doubled: ~60 Mbps.
  • Two people working from home, 4K in the evening, a console that downloads big games. Heaviest pair: 25 + 25 = 50 Mbps. Doubled: ~100 Mbps.
  • Shared house, five or more people, several 4K streams at once. 200–300 Mbps is genuinely useful here.

Beyond roughly 300 Mbps, extra download speed stops changing how the internet feels. It changes how fast large files arrive — a 60 GB game download is 27 minutes at 300 Mbps and 8 minutes at 1 Gbps — and nothing else. If you do not regularly move files that large, gigabit is buying you a faster progress bar.

Upload matters more than the marketing suggests

Providers advertise download speed because it is the bigger number. But upload is what carries your side of a video call, your cloud backups, your photos syncing, and everything you send to a shared drive.

On cable and older DSL connections, upload is a small fraction of download — a 200 Mbps cable plan might give you 10–20 Mbps up. That is usually adequate, but it is the first thing to saturate. A single large backup running in the background can make everyone's video calls stutter, because the acknowledgements that keep downloads flowing are stuck behind your upload queue.

Fibre connections are typically symmetrical, or close to it. If you work from home, upload capacity and a symmetrical connection are worth more than another few hundred Mbps down.

Guideline upload figures

  • 3 Mbps — one HD video call, comfortably.
  • 10 Mbps — several calls, plus background photo sync.
  • 25 Mbps+ — regular large uploads, video editing to the cloud, streaming to Twitch or YouTube.

Why faster plans often do not feel faster

People upgrade, notice no difference, and conclude they were sold a lie. Usually they were not — the bottleneck simply was not their plan. Common culprits:

  • Wi-Fi. An older router or a weak signal will cap you long before your plan does. This is the number one cause by a wide margin, and it is covered in why is my Wi-Fi slow.
  • Latency, not bandwidth. Pages feel slow because of round trips, not throughput. A 1 Gbps satellite connection with 600 ms latency feels dreadful.
  • The server on the other end. A download runs at whatever the source will give you. If a site caps each connection at 5 MB/s, your gigabit line changes nothing.
  • Your device. Old phones, laptops with 2.4 GHz-only Wi-Fi, and cheap USB adapters all have their own ceilings.

A sensible way to decide

  1. Run a speed test on a wired connection if you can, to see what you are getting now.
  2. Work out your realistic heaviest pair of activities and double it.
  3. If what you are getting already exceeds that figure and the internet still feels slow, the plan is not your problem — fix the Wi-Fi or check your latency first.
  4. If you work from home, check the upload number before the download one.

Upgrading a plan that is already fast enough is the most common wasted spend in home internet. Measure before you buy.
